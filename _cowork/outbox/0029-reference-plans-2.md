# What other housing does

Second reference study for the library, written for run 0029. It adds to
`_cowork/outbox/0028-reference-plans.md` rather than replacing it. That study
read ten Swiss and German cooperative plans and found one very consistent
pattern. This one goes looking for the cases that break it.

The reason for widening is in run 0028's own report: the five flats it drew read
as three or four horizontal strips each, and they look like each other. Part of
that is the packer. Part of it is that every plan the first study read was the
same kind of dwelling.

Two plans here were read properly, with dimensions or printed areas. The rest of
the projects are reported from text, and each one says so. Where a plan was
opened and could not be read honestly, that is written down instead of a guess.

## The projects

### Ökohaus, Frei Otto with Hermann Kendel, Berlin-Tiergarten, 1983 to 1992

The closest built precedent to this thesis, and the one the reading spent most
effort on. Three reinforced-concrete tables: columns carrying slabs at 6.00 m
and 12.00 m, leaving two-storey clear voids into which residents built one or
two-storey houses. Frei Otto described it as building plots stacked on top of
each other, and they were sold as serviced plots. Nine architects were involved
and some residents built their own; the project's ecology director records one
teacher who used a sabbatical year to build his flat.

The unit count could not be reconciled. Three sources give 18, 26 and 27. The
best-sourced is 27, from Ekhart Hahn's own project document at
`https://www.ekhart-hahn.de/Content/1/Link/Oekohauuser_Rauchstra%C3%9Fe_Berlin-Tiergarten_.pdf`.

One plan was read with dimensions, House 12's ground floor, published by Solidar
Architekten at
`https://www.solidar-architekten.de/oekohaus-berlin-mitte-tiergarten-corneliusstrasse-11-12/`.
The structural bay is 10.92 m wide by 14.44 m deep. The dwelling inside it is a
skewed quadrilateral of roughly 8 by 14 m whose party walls run at an angle to
the column grid. The printed room areas on that floor are 26.1, 13.5, 11.6 with
5.8, 7.9, 7.6, 5.0, 4.2 and 3.6 with 0.8 m², about 79.5 m² in all, with a stair
marked 16 STG at the east wall.

The rooms are not named on the drawing. They carry areas only, so the names
below are inferred from the fixtures drawn in them and should be read that way.
The 7.9 m² room shows a sink and a hob and is annotated
"OBERLICHT OHNE DIREKTEN AUSBLICK", a rooflight with no direct view: a kitchen
with no facade window at all. The 4.2 m² room shows a WC, a basin and a bath.

Graph, from door swings that were legible:
`entrance and stair → hall (5.0); hall → bath (4.2), room (13.5), kitchen (7.6),
room (11.6), room (7.9); the 7.9 room → living (26.1); living → terrace`.
Circulation on that floor is 5.0 of 79.5 m², about 6.3 per cent, which is at the
bottom of the first study's 5 to 15 per cent range.

What is different: the dwelling is a plot rather than a slot. Its outline is a
free polygon inside a rectangular structural bay, and its kitchen is a toplit
interior room, which none of the ten cooperative plans permit.

### Mehr als Wohnen, Hunziker Areal, Haus A, Duplex Architekten, Zürich, 2015

Run 0028 never reached the Planheft. It was read here. `WebFetch` cannot open it
and the Google Docs viewer route that run 0028 recommended was refused for this
file, so the PDF was downloaded and its pages rendered instead. Sheet A_001,
the first-floor plan at 1:200, carries room areas and flat labels.

Two cluster flats per floor: A.1.1 at 12.5 rooms and 398.9 m², and A.1.2 at 10.5
rooms and 323.5 m², repeated on floors 2 to 5. The house holds five 10.5-room
flats, four 12.5-room flats, one office and one gallery.

The plan is an irregular six-sided ring around a central stair core labelled
Treppenhaus T1, with a second stair T2. The overall dimensions printed on the
sheet are 43.10, 40.54, 31.66, 25.90, 23.75, 21.81, 22.00, 12.10 and 9.85 m.

One cluster is organised as a shared band holding Wohnküche and living rooms of
41.8, 43.1, 47.3 and 48.7 m² and shared halls of 22.5 to 31.7 m², off which hang
ten to twelve private satellites of 13.6 to 22.8 m², each with its own bath of
3.2 to 6.3 m². Named ancillaries are Garderobe and Kiwa at 9.6 and 9.7 m², a
Trockenraum at 10.0, a Waschraum at 10.9, a Büro, a Reduit and a Luftraum.

Graph: `entrance → Garderobe; Garderobe → shared hall; hall → shared Wohnküche,
Büro, Waschraum and each satellite; each satellite → its own bath`.

The sheet states its own rules, which are worth recording because they are the
same family as the app's: escape corridors and stairs at least 1.20 m, internal
stairs at least 0.90 m, window area at least one tenth of floor area in living
and sleeping rooms, and storage of 8 m² per flat, or 5 m² for flats of 2.5 rooms
and under.

What is different: the hall is a room, at 22.5 to 31.7 m², and every private
room has its own bath, so the wet rooms are distributed around the ring rather
than gathered into one block with the kitchen.

### Kalkbreite, Müller Sigrist, Zürich, 2014

Run 0028 opened Kalkbreite and could not read its doors. This time the WBS
object document at
`https://www.bwo.admin.ch/dam/de/sd-web/ryn8k9XN1zNz/WBS%20Objekt%20PDF%20Kalkbreite%20de.pdf`
was read, which carries plan sheets. Its criteria table gives 89 apartments and
269 rooms; other sources say 97 units and 266 residents, and the two were not
reconciled.

The block is a five-sided polygonal perimeter wrapped around a tram depot, with
a raised courtyard over the depot roof at the second floor. The Grosshaushalt
plan on the sixth floor was read at high zoom. Its wall and door geometry is
fully legible and it is a through-block section about 13 to 14 m deep with
private rooms on both opposite facades, a wide central habitable spine that is
not a minimum-width corridor, a middle band of small wet cells serving pairs of
rooms, and one shared kitchen with an island at one end.

**No room-by-room graph is given for it.** The rooms are unnamed and no areas
are printed, so naming which cell is a bath and which is a WC would be a guess.

### Habitat 67, Moshe Safdie, Montreal, 1967

**No plan was read.** The practice page at
`https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/habitat-67` publishes photographs and
text rather than a dimensioned dwelling plan, and none was obtained elsewhere.
The figures below are from text.

354 to 365 modules at 11.7 by 5.3 by 3.0 m, about 62 m² each, making 158
dwellings in 15 types from 56 m² at one bedroom to 167 m² at four. About 6 per
cent of dwellings use one module, 60 per cent use two, 29 per cent three, 4 per
cent four, 1 per cent five, and one custom unit uses eight. Three lift cores,
with lifts stopping every fourth floor onto pedestrian streets off which the
dwellings open directly.

What is different: the dwelling is an assembly of whole rooms as boxes in three
dimensions. Its shape is a stack rather than a footprint, and its terrace is
another unit's roof.

### Habraken, Open Building and the SAR method, with Molenvliet as the built case

The concept splits a building into a support, which the collective fixes, and
infill, which the household decides. The SAR method makes that operational as
zones and margins: bands parallel to the facade, a facade zone, a middle and a
rear, with margins named for the zones they bridge, on a 30 cm tartan grid of 20
plus 10. A room is not placed at a coordinate. It is placed in a zone, and its
boundary floats within a margin.

That is the same split this project makes between the packer and the flat, and
the margin idea is the part worth borrowing: the app currently fixes a room's
rectangle exactly, where SAR fixes a band and lets the edge move.

Molenvliet, by Frans van der Werf at Papendrecht in 1977, is the built case: 123
dwellings from 67 different unit types, with parallel piers, floors and roofs
around courtyards, combining longitudinal and transverse supports, plus a
modular facade system residents chose from.

**No support or infill plan was read.** The zone and margin figures come from a
peer-reviewed article's text at
`https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13467581.2020.1800472`, not from a
drawing. Sources for the rest: `https://www.spatialagency.net/database/john.habraken`
and `https://councilonopenbuilding.org/residential-case-studies/molenvliet`.

### PREVI, Lima, 1969, and Quinta Monroy, Elemental, Iquique, 2003

**No plan was read for either.**

PREVI: 40 ha north of Lima, 1,500 units planned and about 500 built, 24 of 26
schemes built, 13 international and 13 Peruvian teams under Peter Land's master
plan, with built area per house fixed at 60 to 120 m², one to three storeys,
patio type. Stirling's unit is described in prose as four columns defining a
central patio with the living room and bedrooms around it and a second courtyard
at the corner of the plot carrying the kitchen, bathroom and services, starting
as an L-shaped one-floor unit for four people and expandable in plan or by a
second family on top. **That adjacency is derived from prose, not from a
drawing.** Aldo van Eyck put the kitchen at the centre of his plan and shaped
his courtyards with diagonal walls specifically to discourage residents from
building over them.

Quinta Monroy: 93 units in 2003 and 2004 at about USD 7,500 per family covering
land, infrastructure and building. About 30 to 36 m² delivered and about 72 m²
when complete. What is built is precisely the half a family cannot self-build:
structure, bathroom, kitchen, stair and party walls. The other half is a void of
roughly equal footprint between the party walls.

### Kaisersrot, ETH Zürich CAAD with KCAP, from about 2000

**No dwelling plan was found, and there is unlikely to be one.** Kaisersrot
operates at plot and urban-tissue scale: a solver allocates parcels with
individually specified size, proportion, programme, orientation, access and
daylight, and negotiates them against collective constraints such as road length
and shared open space. Partners included Herzog & de Meuron, Sauerbruch Hutton
and KCAP, and it became a start-up in 2008. `kaisersrot.com` refused the
connection and the ETH CAAD wiki returned 403, so this is from search summaries
only and **no built Kaisersrot scheme could be confirmed**.

Its relevance is methodological. It is the closest precedent for treating a
resident's preferences as solver constraints rather than as menu choices, which
is what the building app's packer does.

## What these add that the Swiss cooperative sample does not

Every rule the first study extracted holds only because its sample was drawn
from one dwelling contract, in which a flat is a private, complete,
family-shaped container entered through a threshold. These projects break that
contract in four places, and each break falsifies one of the rules. The entrance
stops being a windowless vestibule, because Hunziker's Haus A enters through a
named Garderobe into a shared hall of 22.5 to 31.7 m² that is a room. Circulation
stops being 5 to 15 per cent, because the shared band of a 398.9 m² cluster is a
large fraction of the flat and is the point of it, while the Ökohaus's private
hall stays at 6.3 per cent precisely because its sharing has moved outside the
front door. Habitable rooms come off the facade, because the Ökohaus kitchen is
toplit and annotated as having no direct view, van Eyck's PREVI kitchen sits at
the centre of the plan, and PREVI and Molenvliet light rooms from private
courtyards. And the en-suite, which the first study could not find once in ten
plans, is the norm in Haus A, where essentially every private room has its own
bath of 3.2 to 6.3 m² and the wet rooms are therefore distributed around the ring
rather than gathered into a block.

The implication for this configurator is that its rules are not architectural
laws. They are the signature of one household model. They should eventually be a
named, switchable set, with a cluster or shared-household set beside the
nuclear-family one, and the four rules above should carry a cost rather than a
rejection. The evidence for that is not theoretical: as the rules stand, the app
cannot express Haus A, which is a built, awarded and thoroughly conventional
Swiss cooperative building.

## Shapes a stack of horizontal bands cannot express

This is the part that shapes the fifteen. Each shape below is named with the
project that shows it and its rough proportions.

A **polygonal ring** that closes. Hunziker Haus A is a six-sided ring about 43 by
40 m overall, wrapped around a central core, each cluster taking two or three
sides. Kalkbreite is a five-sided perimeter. A band model can produce an L by
accident and cannot produce a ring at all.

An **L with a re-entrant corner holding a private patio**. PREVI's Stirling unit
is a one-storey L for four people with a central patio defined by four columns
and a second courtyard at the plot corner, inside a built area of 60 to 120 m².
Bands cannot make a void that is inside the outline and outside the envelope.

A **deep plan with an internal light source**. Ökohaus House 12 is about 8 by 14
m, roughly 1 to 1.75, with a kitchen lit only by a rooflight. A band model puts
every room on an edge by construction.

A **through-flat with two opposite facades and a served central spine**.
Kalkbreite's Grosshaushalt is about 13 to 14 m deep with rooms on both long
facades and the wet cells and spine between them. Hunziker's clusters do this on
every side of the ring at once.

A **non-orthogonal outline**. Ökohaus House 12's dwelling boundary runs at an
angle to its 10.92 by 14.44 m structural bay, and several internal walls follow
the boundary rather than the grid.

**Split-level and interlocking sections**. The Ökohaus's two-storey voids at 6.00
and 12.00 m are filled with one or two-storey houses, so a neighbour's section
can be a different height from yours, and one unit now on the market runs 149 m²
over three levels. Habitat 67 interlocks one to five modules of 11.7 by 5.3 by
3.0 m in three dimensions, 60 per cent of its dwellings using two, which is a
shape with no single floor plan at all.

A **narrow tall section with a deliberate void**. Quinta Monroy is a party-wall
bay of about 30 to 36 m² over two storeys with an equal-area void alongside it
for future growth. Its defining dimension is vertical and its plan is
deliberately half empty.

## Which of these shaped which flat

The fifteen are drawn against this list, and the report for run 0029 records
which of these shapes were reached and which were not. Three are reachable with
the packer as it stands, because they are made of rectangles that happen to be
arranged unusually rather than of geometry the row model cannot describe: the L
with a re-entrant corner, the deep plan with an internal room, and the
through-flat with two opposite facades and a central spine. Two are reachable
only in part: the polygonal ring becomes a C rather than a closed ring, because
closing it needs a row whose slots do not start at the flat's west edge, and
split-level cannot be expressed at all because a storey is a whole storey here.
The non-orthogonal outline is not reachable, since every module is an
axis-aligned rectangle on a 0.6 m grid.
